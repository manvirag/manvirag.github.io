### 1. Insurance in Simple Words

Insurance transfers a large financial risk from you to an insurance company.

You pay a relatively small, predictable amount called a **premium**. In return, the insurer promises to pay according to the policy contract if a covered event occurs.

The most important words are ***according to the policy contract***.

Insurance does not pay simply because something unfortunate happened. It pays when:

- the event is covered;
- the policy is active;
- waiting periods have been completed;
- all required information was disclosed honestly;
- exclusions do not apply;
- claim conditions and documentation requirements are satisfied.

This is why understanding the policy wording is more important than relying only on advertisements, sales pages, or an agent’s verbal promise.

### 2. Term Life Insurance vs Health Insurance

| Area | Term Life Insurance | Health Insurance |
|---|---|---|
| Main purpose | Protects dependants financially after the insured person’s death | Pays eligible medical and hospitalization expenses |
| Benefit type | Usually a fixed lump-sum death benefit | Reimbursement or cashless payment of eligible bills |
| Main question | “What happens financially to my family if I die?” | “How will I pay for treatment if I become ill or injured?” |
| Complexity | Relatively simpler | More complex because of waiting periods, limits, exclusions, room rules, and claim conditions |
| Premium behaviour | Fixed for the chosen policy and payment structure throughout |  Increases over time because of age, medical inflation, and insurer repricing |
| Need for early purchase | Lower premium and easier underwriting | Complete waiting periods early and secure acceptance while healthier |

You may need both policies because they solve different financial problems.

### Part I: Term Life Insurance

### 3. Why Term Insurance Exists

Term insurance protects people who depend on your income.

Imagine that your salary currently supports:

- parents;
- spouse;
- children;
- education goals;
- household expenses;
- loans;
- future family responsibilities.

If you die unexpectedly, your income stops, but those responsibilities may continue. A term insurance payout can help your family replace part of that lost income and meet major financial commitments.

Term insurance is primarily **protection**, not an investment.

A pure term plan normally does not return the premiums if you survive the policy term. That is not a defect. The premium buys protection during the covered period, in the same way that a health insurance premium buys medical protection even when no claim occurs.

---

### 4. Who Needs Term Insurance?

You generally need term insurance when someone would face financial difficulty because of your death.

Possible dependants include:

- financially dependent parents;
- a spouse who depends fully or partly on your income;
- children;
- siblings or other family members who depend on you;
- a co-borrower who may struggle with a joint loan after your death.

You may need less or no term insurance when:

- nobody depends on your income;
- you have no meaningful debt;
- your family already has enough liquid assets to remain financially secure;
- your accumulated assets can independently replace your future financial contribution.

However, dependency can change after marriage, childbirth, a home loan, or retirement planning for parents. Review your cover after major life events.

Employer-provided group life cover, provident fund balances, investments, and other assets can help  ( that's why its important to add nominees ), but they should not automatically be treated as a complete substitute for personal term insurance . Employer cover may end when you leave the job.

---

### 5. How Much Cover to Buy

Do not choose the sum assured only because a round number sounds large.

Estimate the financial gap your family would face.

A simple framework is:

```text
Required term cover
= Income replacement needed
+ Outstanding loans
+ Major future goals
+ Emergency reserve
− Existing assets available for dependants
− Existing life insurance
```

Consider:

- annual household expenses;
- number and age of dependants;
- parents’ expected retirement needs;
- spouse’s current and future income;
- children’s education;
- major loans;
- inflation;
- investments and liquid assets;
- existing employer or personal life cover.

The **sum assured** is the amount payable under the policy when the insured event occurs, subject to policy conditions.

Do not confuse:

- **sum assured** with premium;
- **policy term** with premium payment term;
- **life cover** with investment maturity value.

---

### 6. How Long the Policy Should Continue

The policy should normally continue until your financial dependants are expected to become independent or until your assets become sufficient to replace your income.

For example, someone aged 26 may consider cover until approximately age 65–70 when:

- parents’ financial needs have been addressed;
- children, if any, are likely to be independent;
- major loans are expected to be repaid;
- retirement assets have been accumulated.

A longer term is not automatically better. You are paying for coverage during years in which your death would create a financial gap.

Ask:

1. Until what age will my income be essential?
2. When will my major liabilities end?
3. By what age should my investments be enough for my family?
4. Will my children be financially independent by then?

### 7. Premium Payment Options

##### 7.1 Regular Pay

Under regular pay, you pay premiums throughout the premium payment term, often for the entire policy term.

Example:

- policy term: 30 years;
- premium payment term: 30 years.

Advantages:

- lower annual premium;
- cost is spread over many years;
- more flexibility if your needs change later.

Disadvantages:

- you must continue paying for a long period;
- missing premiums can cause the policy to lapse.

##### 7.2 Limited Pay

Under limited pay, you finish paying premiums earlier while the cover continues for the full policy term.

Example:

- policy term: 30 years;
- premium payment term: 10 or 15 years.

Advantages:

- premium obligation ends earlier, mentally free.
- may suit someone who expects early retirement or uncertain future income.

Disadvantages:

- much higher annual premium;
- the total economic cost may be higher;
- money is paid earlier instead of remaining available for investment or emergencies.

##### 7.3 Which Is Better?

Regular pay is often easier and more flexible for a beginner because the annual burden is lower. Limited pay may make sense when finishing premiums early is an important personal objective and the higher payment is comfortably affordable.

### 8. How to Compare Term Insurers

No single ratio can tell you which insurer is best. Use several indicators together and then examine the actual product wording.

##### 8.1 Claim Settlement Ratio

The claim settlement ratio usually indicates the percentage of claims settled by count.

A high ratio is generally reassuring, but it can be misleading when viewed alone. An insurer may settle many small claims while rejecting a few very large ones. But term life usually has good ratio as compare to health , since having fraud in this is relative less probable.

Use it as a screening metric, not as the final decision.

##### 8.2 Amount Settlement Ratio

The amount settlement ratio focuses on the value of claims paid rather than only the number of claims.

Illustration:

- 100 claims are received;
- 99 claims of ₹10,000 each are paid;
- one claim of ₹50 lakh is rejected.

The count-based settlement ratio appears very high, but a large portion of the total money claimed was not paid. This is why the amount-based ratio matters.

##### 8.3 Solvency Ratio

The solvency ratio indicates whether an insurer has enough capital relative to its liabilities and risks.

A higher solvency margin can provide comfort, but an extremely high ratio does not automatically mean better service or claims experience. Check the current regulatory requirement and the insurer’s latest disclosed ratio.

##### 8.4 Business Scale and Stability

Consider:

- annual premium income;
- years in business;
- financial strength;
- scale of operations;
- service capability.


##### 8.5 Complaint and Service Data

Review:

- check complain ratio its like in 10k , how many complaints were raised. 
- claim servicing experience;
- turnaround times;
- grievance handling;
- digital service;
- branch and support availability.

##### 8.6 Product Wording

Two insurers with similar ratios can offer different terms.

Check:

- exclusions;
- definitions;
- payout options;
- premium guarantees;
- riders;
- lapse and revival rules;
- medical underwriting;
- claim notification requirements;
- nominee provisions;
- dangerous occupation and activity clauses.
- lock in period rules and refunds

> **Do not select an insurer only because it appears in a “top picks” list.** Shortlist companies, then read the exact policy documents for the product you are considering. And make sure its suitable for you.

### 10. Disclosures, Occupation, and Lifestyle

Honest disclosure is one of the most important parts of buying insurance.

Declare:

- smoking, including occasional smoking;
- tobacco or nicotine use;
- alcohol consumption;
- current and past illnesses;
- medicines;
- surgeries;
- hospitalizations;
- abnormal test results;
- ongoing investigations;
- risky occupation;
- hazardous hobbies or adventure sports;
- existing and pending insurance applications.

Do not allow an agent to answer medical questions casually on your behalf.

Read every answer in the proposal form before submitting it.

##### Occupation Risk

Insurers may classify occupations into lower, medium, or higher risk.

Examples:

- lower risk: office employee, IT professional, teacher, accountant;
- medium risk: field sales, professional driving, factory supervision;
- higher risk: construction, mining, firefighting, active defence duties, oil rigs.

A higher-risk occupation may result in:

- higher premium;
- additional checks;
- limits;
- special terms;
- rejection in extreme cases.

##### Lifestyle Risk

Smoking, heavy alcohol use, certain sports, and other lifestyle factors can affect premium and underwriting. Hiding them creates claim risk.

### 11. Term Insurance Riders

A rider is an optional benefit added to the base policy for an additional premium.

Common riders include:

##### Critical Illness Rider

Pays a fixed amount when a listed critical illness meets the rider’s definition and conditions.

Check:

- list of covered illnesses;
- survival period;
- waiting period;
- severity requirements;
- whether payment reduces the main life cover;
- whether the rider ends after payment.

##### Accidental Death Rider

Pays an additional amount if death results from a covered accident.

Check the definition of accident and exclusions.

##### Waiver of Premium Rider

Future premiums may be waived after a covered disability or critical event while the base cover continues.

##### Disability Income Rider

May pay regular income after a qualifying disability.

##### Return of Premium Option

Returns specified premiums if the insured survives the term.

This is usually not a pure rider in every product and may substantially increase premium. Compare it with buying a simple term plan and investing the difference separately.

###### Should You Add Riders?

Do not add every rider automatically.

Ask:

- Is the benefit meaningful?
- Is the definition narrow?
- Is the payout enough?
- Does it duplicate health, accident, or disability insurance?
- Would a separate policy offer better coverage?
- Does the rider continue for the full policy term?

### 12. Exclusions and Suicide Clause

##### Exclusions

Exclusions describe events or circumstances for which the insurer will not pay, or will pay only under limited conditions.

Read the exact policy wording for:

- suicide;
- fraud or misrepresentation;
- hazardous activities;
- war or civil disturbance;
- aviation;
- criminal acts;
- occupation-related restrictions;
- rider-specific exclusions.

A pure term policy may have relatively few exclusions in written ( so above thing which i told they don't usually written for you base sum insured, make sure to explicitly ask and take in written over email or in policy. they make this dicy. ), but riders can have many more.

##### Suicide Clause

Policies commonly contain a special rule for suicide within an initial period, often the first 12 months from commencement or revival.

Instead of the full sum assured, the policy may pay a limited amount or refund a specified percentage of premiums according to the contract.

After the initial exclusion period, the treatment of a suicide claim depends on the policy wording and applicable rules.

Do not rely on a simplified statement such as “full payment after one year” without reading the exact clause.

### 13. Lapse, Grace Period, Revival

##### Grace Period

The grace period is additional time after the premium due date during which you can pay the premium.

The exact length depends on the premium frequency and policy terms.

##### Lapse

If the premium is not paid within the grace period, the policy may lapse.

For a pure term policy, a lapsed policy usually means:

- life cover stops;
- no normal death benefit is payable during the lapse;
- the family may receive nothing unless a specific provision applies.

##### Revival

A lapsed policy may be revived within the permitted revival period.

The insurer may require:

- unpaid premiums;
- interest or late charges;
- a health declaration;
- new medical tests;
- fresh underwriting;
- evidence of continued insurability.

Revival is not always automatic.

Use auto-debit, calendar reminders, and updated contact details to avoid accidental lapse.

### Part II: Health Insurance ( In this section we'll learn different terminology and their details. )

### 16. Why Health Insurance Is More Complex

Health insurance contains more moving parts than term insurance.

A ₹10 lakh policy does not necessarily mean that the insurer will pay every hospital bill up to ₹10 lakh.

The actual payment can be affected by:

- waiting periods;
- room-rent limits;
- ICU limits;
- co-pay;
- deductible;
- disease-wise caps;
- sub-limits;
- exclusions;
- non-medical items;
- network status;
- treatment definitions;
- pre-authorization;
- documentation;
- medical necessity;
- restoration conditions;
- remaining sum insured.

Therefore, never compare health policies only by premium and total sum insured.

### 17. Retail, Group, Individual, and Floater Plans

##### 17.1 Group Health Insurance

A group policy is usually provided through an employer, association, or other organization.

Advantages:

- often inexpensive or employer-paid;
- usually cover pre-existing diseases with shorter or no waiting period; ( in case of employer mostly it do , check this. )
- useful as additional cover.

Limitations:

- cover can end when you leave the organization; ( so now if you buy , your waiting period or moratorium period ( later ). )
- employer can change insurer or benefits;
- sum insured may be insufficient;
- parents may be excluded or costly;
- policy terms are controlled by the group master policyholder.
- non-controllable.

Do not rely only on employer insurance for lifelong protection.

##### 17.2 Retail Health Insurance

A retail policy is purchased by you directly for yourself or family.

Advantages:

- continues independently of employment;
- portable or migratable subject to rules;
- you choose the cover and features;
- builds continuity and waiting-period credit and moratorium period.

##### 17.3 Individual Policy

Each insured person has a separate sum insured.

Example:

- Person A: ₹10 lakh;
- Person B: ₹10 lakh.

This can be useful when:

- ages differ significantly;
- one person has higher health risk;
- parents need separate coverage;
- you do not want one member’s claim to consume the whole family cover.

##### 17.4 Family Floater

One sum insured is shared by all covered members.

Example:

- family floater: ₹10 lakh;
- any covered member can use it;
- total claims in the year generally cannot exceed the available shared cover, subject to restoration and policy rules.

Floaters can be efficient for a young family, but combining young adults and older parents in the same floater can be inefficient because:

- premium may be based heavily on the oldest member;
- parents may consume most of the shared cover;
- simultaneous claims create pressure on the shared sum insured.

A common structure is:

- one floater for self, spouse, and children;
- separate individual or floater coverage for parents.

### 19. How to Evaluate a Health Insurer

Use insurer-level metrics only as screening tools. Product wording and claim conditions remain essential.

##### 19.1 Claim Settlement Ratio

Shows the percentage of claims settled by count.

A high ratio is useful, but it does not show:

- how much money was paid;
- how many claims were partially paid;
- whether claims were delayed;
- whether cashless requests were denied but reimbursement later succeeded.

##### 19.2 Incurred Claim Ratio

The incurred claim ratio compares claims incurred with premium earned over a period.

Very low values may suggest that a relatively small part of premium is being used for claims, though product mix and accounting matter.

Very high values may indicate pressure on profitability and possible future repricing, but one year alone is not enough for a conclusion.

Study the trend over several years.

##### 19.3 Amount Settlement Ratio

Shows how much of the total claimed amount was settled.

It helps identify whether the insurer pays a high number of small claims but rejects or reduces large claims.

##### 19.4 Complaint Ratio

Shows grievances relative to the policyholder base, often expressed per 10,000 policies or claims depending on the source.

Compare insurers using the same definition and reporting period.

##### 19.5 Solvency and Long-term Stability

Health insurance is a long-term relationship. Consider whether the insurer is financially strong and likely to service policies decades later.

Review:

- solvency;
- business scale;
- branch and digital support;
- grievance record.

##### 19.6 Network Strength

A large network count is not enough.

Check whether the hospitals you would realistically use are included, especially:

- near your home;
- in your parents’ city;
- tertiary hospitals;
- hospitals for likely specialties;
- emergency facilities.

Network lists can change, so verify close to purchase and again before planned hospitalization.

### 20. Hospital Network, Cashless, and Reimbursement

##### Cashless Claim

At a network hospital, the insurer or its claims administrator may directly settle approved eligible expenses with the hospital.

You may still need to pay:

- deductibles;
- co-pay;
- non-medical items;
- excluded expenses;
- costs above sub-limits;
- room-related deductions;
- unapproved amounts.

Cashless is not the same as “everything is free.”

##### Reimbursement Claim

At a non-network hospital, or when cashless is not approved, you may pay the hospital and submit documents for reimbursement.

A cashless denial does not always mean the final claim is invalid. Reimbursement may still be possible if the treatment is covered and documentation is complete.

Check:

- claim notification deadline;
- pre-authorization process;
- emergency admission process;
- document list;
- submission deadline;
- original-document requirements;
- reimbursement turnaround time.

---

### 21. What Counts as Hospitalization?

Hospitalization generally means formal admission to a recognized hospital for medically necessary treatment under a doctor’s advice.

A policy may require ( these are sample, depend on policy wording ):

- an allotted bed;
- inpatient records;
- a minimum stay, commonly 24 hours; ( or some 2h)
- recognized hospital facilities;
- treatment rather than diagnostic observation alone.

Exceptions may include listed day-care procedures that require less than 24 hours because of modern technology.

Usually not treated as inpatient hospitalization:

- routine OPD visit;
- diagnostic visit without treatment admission;
- treatment taken only for convenience;
- non-medically necessary admission.

Always read the policy definition of:

- hospital;
- hospitalization;
- medical practitioner;
- medically necessary treatment;
- inpatient care;
- day-care centre;
- intensive care unit.

### 22. Waiting Periods

A waiting period is the time during which certain claims are not covered after the policy begins.

##### 22.1 Initial Waiting Period

Many policies have an initial waiting period, often around 30 days, for non-accidental illnesses.

Accidents are commonly covered from the start, subject to the policy.

##### 22.2 Pre-existing Disease Waiting Period

A pre-existing disease is determined according to the policy and regulatory definition.

Examples may include:

- diabetes;
- blood pressure;
- thyroid disorder;
- asthma;
- prior heart conditions.

The insurer may cover them only after a specified continuous waiting period and you told them :), if you are having already something , else they wont' do. even after moratorium period telling this as fruad ( reddit exp. )

##### 22.3 Specific Disease or Procedure Waiting Period

Certain listed conditions may have a separate waiting period even if they were not present before purchase.

Examples can include:

- hernia;
- cataract;
- piles;
- joint replacement;
- certain ENT or gynaecological conditions.

The exact list varies by policy.

##### 22.4 Maternity Waiting Period

Maternity cover may have a long waiting period and a separate benefit limit.

Planning early is important if maternity cover is required.

##### Why Buy Early When Premium Still Increases?

Buying early helps you:

- complete waiting periods before illness develops;
- secure acceptance while healthier;
- reduce the risk of loading, co-pay, or rejection;
- build no-claim bonus;
- establish continuity;
- avoid a future disease being treated as pre-existing at first purchase.

The premium may still rise with age and medical inflation, but delaying can add restrictions as well as cost. ( but some provide some fetaure like Niva Bupa gave, Lock the clock -> no increase until no claim. )

### 23. Room Rent, ICU Limits, and Proportionate Deduction

##### Room Rent Limit

A room-rent limit is the maximum eligible room charge per day or permitted room category.

It can be expressed as:

- fixed amount per day;
- percentage of sum insured;
- specified room category;
- no separate limit.

##### Note:: Proportionate Deduction

Choosing a room above the eligible limit can reduce not only the room charge but also associated expenses.

Example:

- eligible room: ₹5,000 per day;
- chosen room: ₹10,000 per day;
- eligibility ratio: 50%.

Depending on the policy, associated charges such as surgeon, doctor, nursing, and procedure fees may be reduced proportionately.

This can create a large out-of-pocket expense.

Prefer:

- no room-rent monetary limit;
- no restrictive room category;
- wording that clearly avoids proportionate deductions.

##### ICU Limit

ICU limits may be separate from room-rent limits. usually it doesn't have some limit. 

Check:

- whether ICU has no limit;
- whether proportionate deduction applies;
- whether ventilator and critical-care charges are included.

“No room-rent limit” does not automatically mean “no ICU limit.” But it usually have no limit on ICU ( no limit here mean , upto sum insured. )

### 24. Sub-limits and Disease-wise Caps

##### Sub-limit

A sub-limit is a smaller cap within the overall sum insured.

Examples:

- ambulance;
- cataract;
- maternity;
- ICU;
- room rent;
- modern treatments;
- durable equipment.

Example:

- total sum insured: ₹5 lakh;
- cataract sub-limit: ₹40,000;
- cataract bill: ₹70,000;
- insurer pays up to the eligible ₹40,000;
- you pay the remaining amount, subject to other conditions.

##### Disease-wise Cap

A disease-wise cap sets a maximum payment for a particular illness or treatment.

Common examples in some policies:

- cataract;
- hernia;
- piles;
- knee replacement;
- tonsillectomy.

A ₹10 lakh policy with a ₹50,000 disease cap does not provide ₹10 lakh for that treatment.

Prefer policies with:

- no disease-wise caps;
- few sub-limits;
- high and practical limits where caps exist.

Senior citizen and low-premium plans may contain more restrictions, so read them carefully.

### 25. Co-pay and Deductible

These terms are different.

##### Co-pay

Co-pay is the percentage of every eligible claim that you must pay.

Example:

- eligible bill: ₹1,00,000;
- co-pay: 10%;
- you pay: ₹10,000;
- insurer pays: ₹90,000, subject to other deductions.

Co-pay can apply:

- to all claims;
- only above a certain age;
- at non-network hospitals;
- outside a zone;
- for specified diseases;
- when chosen voluntarily for premium discount.

Avoid mandatory co-pay where affordable, especially for parents, because repeated claims can create significant out-of-pocket cost.

##### Deductible

A deductible is the amount that must be borne by you or another policy before the insurer starts paying for each hospitalisation/claim.

Deductibles are common in top-up. Super top-up policies ( super top-up usually have aggregate, check below ).

Example:

- deductible: ₹5 lakh;
- eligible annual claim amount: ₹8 lakh;
- top-up layer may pay ₹3 lakh, depending on whether it is a top-up or super top-up and other terms.

### 26. Restoration Benefit and No-Claim Bonus

These features are often confused.

##### Restoration Benefit

Restoration refills the sum insured after it has been used, according to policy rules.

Check:

- whether restoration activates after partial or full exhaustion;
- whether it can be used for the same illness;
- whether it can be used by the same insured member;
- whether it can be used for the same hospitalization;
- how many times it restores;
- whether it is automatic;
- whether it applies to base cover only;
- whether it is available after a single large claim;
- whether restored cover carries forward.

Restoration usually applies to future claims in the same policy year and normally does not carry forward.

It also may not increase the maximum available for one hospitalization.

##### No-Claim Bonus or Cumulative Bonus

A no-claim bonus rewards claim-free years by increasing cover or, in some products, reducing premium.

Check:

- annual increase;
- maximum bonus;
- whether a claim reduces or resets it;
- whether bonus protection exists;
- whether it applies to the base sum insured;
- whether it transfers during portability.

Example:

- base cover: ₹5 lakh;
- bonus after renewal: ₹6 lakh;
- later: ₹7 lakh;
- subject to the product’s maximum.

###### Key Difference

- **Restoration:** refills used cover during the policy year.
- **No-claim bonus:** grows cover over claim-free renewals.

Do not count both as guaranteed base cover for every claim.

### 27. Top-up vs Super Top-up

Both provide additional cover above a deductible.

##### Top-up Plan

A top-up usually tests the deductible against each individual claim.

Example:

- deductible: ₹5 lakh;
- claim 1: ₹4 lakh → does not cross deductible;
- claim 2: ₹4 lakh → also does not cross deductible;
- no top-up payout merely because total claims are ₹8 lakh.

If one eligible claim is ₹6 lakh:

- deductible: ₹5 lakh;
- top-up portion may pay ₹1 lakh, subject to terms.

##### Super Top-up Plan

A super top-up usually aggregates eligible claims during the policy year.

Example:

- deductible: ₹5 lakh;
- claim 1: ₹3 lakh;
- claim 2: ₹3 lakh;
- annual aggregate: ₹6 lakh;
- super top-up may pay ₹1 lakh above the deductible.

##### Why Super Top-up Is Usually More Useful

It can protect against multiple hospitalizations in one year.

A common structure is:

```text
Base policy: ₹5–10 lakh
Super top-up: additional ₹10–20 lakh or more
Deductible: matched to reliable base cover or affordable self-funding
```

Before buying, check:

- whether deductible is annual aggregate;
- whether all claims count;
- whether different family members’ claims aggregate;
- whether pre-existing disease waiting period applies separately;
- whether room limits and exclusions differ from the base policy;
- whether the base and super top-up can be from different insurers;
- whether you must first use another policy;
- claim coordination process.

A super top-up is not a substitute for a good base policy.

### 28. Day Care, OPD, Home Care, and Domiciliary Treatment

##### Day-Care Treatment

A day-care procedure is a medically necessary procedure that does not require 24-hour hospitalization because of technological advancement.

Examples may include:

- cataract surgery;
- dialysis;
- chemotherapy;
- radiotherapy;
- certain minor surgeries.

It is different from ordinary OPD treatment.

Check whether the policy covers:

- all medically necessary day-care procedures;
- only a listed set;
- day-care centre requirements;
- procedure-specific caps.

##### OPD Cover

OPD refers to treatment without hospital admission.

Possible expenses:

- doctor consultation;
- medicines;
- diagnostic tests;
- routine follow-up.

OPD cover is often:

- optional;
- limited to a small annual amount;
- reimbursement-based;
- subject to prescriptions and bills;
- expensive relative to the benefit.

Do not choose a weak hospitalization policy only because it offers attractive OPD benefits.

##### Domiciliary Hospitalization

Domiciliary hospitalization is hospital-level treatment at home under special conditions, such as:

- the patient cannot be moved;
- a hospital bed is unavailable;
- a doctor prescribes treatment at home;
- the treatment is medically necessary.

It is not ordinary home rest or convenience treatment.

##### Home Care Treatment

Some policies separately cover listed treatments administered at home that would otherwise require hospitalization.

Check definitions carefully because **home care** and **domiciliary hospitalization** may have different eligibility rules.

### 29. Consumables and Non-medical Items

Hospital bills often include non-medical or consumable items such as:

- gloves;
- masks;
- PPE kits;
- syringes;
- cotton;
- bandages;
- administrative supplies.

Basic policies may exclude many of these items.

Some plans include a consumables add-on or in-built cover.

Check:

- the exact annexure of excluded items;
- which items are covered by the add-on;
- whether all lists are covered;
- whether the item must appear as part of room, procedure, or treatment charges;
- whether separate billing makes it ineligible;
- whether there is a cap.

Non-medical expenses can form a meaningful part of the bill, particularly during intensive treatment. For parents, consumables cover may be more valuable than for a healthy young buyer, but the exact benefit should be compared with its premium.

### 30. Other Important Health Insurance Features

##### 30.1 Pre- and Post-Hospitalization

###### Pre-Hospitalization

Covers eligible expenses before admission related to the same illness, such as:

- doctor consultations;
- diagnostic tests;
- medicines.

A common period may be 30–60 days, but verify the policy.

###### Post-Hospitalization

Covers eligible recovery expenses after discharge related to the same hospitalization, such as:

- follow-up visits;
- medicines;
- tests;
- prescribed physiotherapy.

A common period may be 60–180 days, but verify the policy.

The key condition is usually that the expenses must relate to the covered hospitalization.

##### 30.2 Maternity Cover

Check:

- waiting period;
- normal delivery limit;
- caesarean limit;
- newborn cover;
- vaccination cover;
- infertility exclusions;
- number-of-delivery restrictions;
- whether maternity expenses reduce the main sum insured.

A ₹10 lakh policy may have only a ₹50,000 maternity sub-limit.

##### 30.3 Modern Treatments

Modern treatments can include advanced procedures such as:

- robotic surgery;
- laser procedures;
- oral chemotherapy;
- balloon sinuplasty;
- deep brain stimulation;
- stem-cell therapy in specified cases.

Check:

- exact list;
- regulatory minimum coverage;
- sub-limits;
- medical indications;
- exclusions.

##### 30.4 AYUSH Cover

AYUSH refers to:

- Ayurveda;
- Yoga and Naturopathy;
- Unani;
- Siddha;
- Homeopathy.

Coverage may require treatment at an eligible recognized AYUSH hospital.

Check:

- inpatient requirement;
- hospital recognition;
- treatment limits;
- exclusions;
- whether outpatient AYUSH is covered.

##### 30.5 Organ Donor Cover

May cover eligible expenses of the donor’s hospitalization and surgery when the insured recipient receives the organ.

Check:

- donor screening;
- donor pre- and post-hospitalization;
- complications;
- organ harvesting;
- sub-limits;
- whether multiple potential donors’ tests are covered.

The cost of purchasing an organ is illegal and not covered.

##### 30.6 Ambulance Cover

Check:

- road ambulance limit;
- emergency requirement;
- hospital-to-hospital transfer;
- documentation;
- air ambulance conditions and cap.

Road ambulance is useful; air ambulance may be unnecessary for many buyers unless location and medical access make it relevant.

##### 30.7 Second Medical Opinion

Provides access to another specialist’s opinion for a major diagnosis or treatment.

It may help avoid:

- unnecessary surgery;
- incorrect diagnosis;
- inappropriate or excessively expensive treatment.

Check whether it is advisory only and whether treatment costs remain subject to the normal policy.

##### 30.8 Wellness Benefits

May provide rewards for:

- steps;
- exercise;
- gym activity;
- yoga;
- health assessments;
- preventive check-ups.

Rewards may include:

- points;
- vouchers;
- premium discount;
- additional sum insured.

Treat wellness benefits as secondary. They should not outweigh core hospitalization coverage.

##### 30.9 Preventive Health Check-up

Check:

- frequency;
- test package;
- network centres;
- cashless or reimbursement method;
- whether it is available every year or only after claim-free years;
- whether unused benefits carry forward.

##### 30.10 Geographical Coverage

Most Indian retail health policies primarily cover treatment in India.

Worldwide cover, where offered, may be limited to:

- emergencies;
- selected countries;
- specified treatments;
- reimbursement only;
- strict pre-authorization.

Do not assume overseas treatment is covered unless the policy clearly says so.

### 31. Health Insurance Exclusions

Exclusions are among the most important sections of the policy.

##### Permanent Exclusions

These may never be covered under the policy.

Examples can include:

- cosmetic treatment without medical necessity;
- self-inflicted injury;
- certain fertility treatment;
- treatment outside policy definitions;
- illegal acts;
- non-medical services.

##### Time-bound Exclusions

These may become covered after a waiting period.

Examples:

- pre-existing diseases;
- specified illnesses;
- maternity.

##### Common Areas to Check

- dental treatment;
- vision correction;
- obesity and weight-management treatment;
- infertility;
- maternity;
- OPD;
- experimental treatment;
- unproven treatment;
- substance abuse;
- self-harm;
- war and nuclear risks;
- treatment abroad;
- non-medical items;
- admission only for investigation;
- rehabilitation and respite care;
- congenital conditions;
- psychiatric treatment;
- durable medical equipment.

Do not assume a condition is excluded merely because it is not listed in the brochure. Read:

1. permanent exclusions;
2. standard exclusions;
3. specific exclusions;
4. benefit-specific exclusions;
5. waiting-period section;
6. definitions.

### 32. Renewal, Premium Increases, and Loading

##### Lifetime Renewability

Choose a policy with lifetime renewability.

Old age is when health insurance is most needed. A policy that forces exit at a certain age can leave you unprotected when buying a new policy becomes difficult.

Check the exact renewal clause.

##### Premium Increases

Health insurance premium can rise because of:

- moving to a higher age band;
- medical inflation;
- insurer-wide repricing;
- tax changes;
- product revision;
- increased sum insured;
- added members;
- optional benefits.

Budget for future premiums, not only the first-year price.

##### Loading

Loading is an additional premium charged because of higher health risk.

It may be applied due to:

- diabetes;
- hypertension;
- heart condition;
- obesity;
- abnormal tests;
- other medical history.

Possible underwriting decisions include:

- loading;
- co-pay;
- exclusions where permitted;
- waiting periods;
- decline.

Do not hide illness to avoid loading. A correctly issued policy at a higher premium is safer than a cheaper policy based on false information.

### 33. Portability,  Migration and Moratorium Period

##### Portability

Portability means moving from one insurer to another while seeking continuity credit for completed waiting periods and other eligible benefits.

Possible advantages:

- carry forward waiting-period credit;
- move to a better product or service provider;
- preserve continuity up to eligible limits.

Important limitations:

- new insurer can underwrite again;
- acceptance is not guaranteed;
- premium and features can change;
- increased sum insured may face new waiting periods;
- exact no-claim bonus treatment varies;
- documents and timelines matter.

Apply sufficiently before renewal according to current rules and insurer process. Do not allow a break in coverage.

##### Migration

Migration generally means moving from one product to another within the same insurer, subject to applicable rules.

It may be useful when:

- an old product is discontinued;
- a better product becomes available;
- you want different features or cover.

##### Moratorium Period

The moratorium period is the continuous period after which an insurer generally cannot question a policy for past non-disclosure, except in cases of proven fraud ( and they take PED as fraud ref: reddit, in case you already know , but didn't tell. ). 

It may be useful because:

* it provides greater protection after continuous coverage;
* it generally applies after 5 continuous years;
* portability or migration may help retain continuity benefits.

Read how continuity, bonus, waiting periods, and underwriting are handled.

### 34. Common Reasons for Claim Rejection

##### 34.1 Non-disclosure or Misrepresentation

One of the biggest risks is hiding or incorrectly declaring:

- disease;
- medicines;
- consultations;
- past hospitalization;
- abnormal reports;
- smoking;
- alcohol use;
- previous insurance rejection;
- existing policies.

The insurer may investigate medical history during a claim.

##### 34.2 Waiting Period Not Completed

A claim can be denied when:

- the initial waiting period applies;
- a pre-existing disease waiting period is incomplete;
- the illness is on a specific waiting list;
- maternity waiting is incomplete.

##### 34.3 Treatment Is Excluded

The policy may exclude:

- a treatment category;
- a non-medical item;
- an experimental procedure;
- OPD;
- cosmetic treatment;
- other specified events.

##### 34.4 Definition Not Met

Examples:

- facility does not meet the policy’s definition of hospital;
- procedure does not qualify as day care;
- admission was for investigation only;
- treatment was not medically necessary;
- domiciliary conditions were not met.

##### 34.5 Missing or Incorrect Documents

Possible issues:

- late notification;
- missing discharge summary;
- missing prescriptions;
- incomplete bills;
- no diagnostic evidence;
- inconsistent records;
- failure to submit original documents when required.

##### 34.6 Partial Payment Rather Than Full Rejection

Claims may be reduced because of:

- room-rent proportionate deduction;
- co-pay;
- deductible;
- sub-limit;
- disease cap;
- non-medical items;
- exhausted sum insured;
- ineligible charges.

Always ask for a written deduction sheet and reason when the approved amount is lower than expected.

### 35. Buying Directly vs Using an Intermediary

An intermediary may be:

- insurance agent;
- broker;
- online advisory platform;
- corporate agent;
- other licensed distributor.

##### Benefits of an Intermediary

- explains unfamiliar terms;
- helps compare products;
- assists with proposal forms;
- may support claims;
- useful for parents or complex cases.

##### Risks

- commission bias;
- pressure to buy a particular insurer;
- poor-quality advice;
- verbal promises that do not appear in the contract;
- agent filling incorrect answers.

##### Buying Directly

Advantages:

- direct control;
- no reliance on a salesperson;
- you review documents yourself.
- no personal information sharing to agent.
- Learning.

Disadvantages:

- you must understand the policy;
- you handle comparisons and claims;
- you may miss hidden conditions.

The premium is not necessarily lower when buying direct.

Never sign or submit a proposal form you have not reviewed.

- low waiting periods;
- broad hospitalization cover;
- restoration that can be used practically;
- strong cumulative bonus;
- long-term affordability;
- portability;
- hospitals near present and likely future locations.

OPD, wellness, maternity, and global cover should be selected only when genuinely needed.

##### 39.3 Term Insurance Template

Consider:

- current dependent parents;
- future spouse or children;
- loans;
- expected working age;
- retirement assets;
- existing employer cover;
- regular-pay affordability;
- nominee updates after marriage;
- family access to policy documents.

Recalculate after major life events instead of blindly buying multiple overlapping plans.

### 41. Common Beginner Mistakes

##### Mistake 1: Buying Only Because the Premium Is Low

A cheap plan may contain:

- co-pay;
- room cap;
- disease cap;
- long waiting period;
- restricted network;
- weak restoration;
- many sub-limits.

##### Mistake 2: Comparing Only Claim Settlement Ratio

Use multiple metrics and the product wording.

##### Mistake 3: Depending Only on Employer Insurance

Employment can change. Retail continuity is valuable.

##### Mistake 4: Hiding a Disease or Habit

A lower premium is meaningless if the claim is later disputed.

##### Mistake 5: Believing a ₹10 Lakh Policy Pays Every ₹10 Lakh Bill

Limits, exclusions, co-pay, non-medical items, and room deductions can reduce payment.

##### Mistake 6: Treating Restoration as Unlimited Base Cover

Restoration may not work for the same hospitalization, same illness, or same member.

##### Mistake 7: Confusing Top-up with Super Top-up

Top-up generally looks at one claim; super top-up generally aggregates annual claims.

##### Mistake 8: Ignoring Future Premium

Check affordability at older age bands, especially for parents.

##### Mistake 9: Selecting a Plan for Wellness or OPD Perks

Core hospitalization terms matter much more.

##### Mistake 10: Not Reading the Proposal Form

Incorrect answers entered by an agent still create risk for the policyholder.

##### Mistake 11: Not Checking Local Hospitals

A large national network is not useful when your preferred local hospital is absent.

##### Mistake 12: Missing Renewal

A break can affect cover and continuity. Set multiple reminders.

##### Mistake 13: Buying a Return-of-Premium Term Plan Without Comparing Alternatives

Compare the total extra premium with a pure term policy plus separate investments.

##### Mistake 14: Buying Too Many Riders

A rider is valuable only when its definition, payout, and duration solve a real need.

##### Mistake 15: Assuming Every Online Recommendation Is Current

Products, ratios, taxes, hospital networks, and policy terms change.

### 42. Final Summary

##### Term Life Insurance

Buy term insurance to protect dependants from the financial loss caused by your death.

Focus on:

- adequate sum assured;
- appropriate policy term;
- affordable payment structure;
- honest disclosure;
- insurer stability;
- claim and amount settlement data;
- exclusions;
- nominee and family preparedness.

Term insurance is usually simpler than health insurance, but the proposal form and disclosures are critical.

##### Health Insurance

Buy health insurance early to build continuity and complete waiting periods while you are healthier.

Focus on:

- strong retail base cover;
- logical family structure;
- super top-up for catastrophic costs;
- no room-rent or ICU restrictions;
- minimal co-pay and sub-limits;
- manageable waiting periods;
- practical restoration;
- local hospital network;
- lifetime renewability;
- complete medical disclosure;
- long-term affordability.

##### The Most Important Rule

> **The brochure explains why you may want the policy. The policy wording explains when the insurer must pay.**

Read the wording, ask questions in writing, disclose everything, and ensure your family knows how to use the policy.
